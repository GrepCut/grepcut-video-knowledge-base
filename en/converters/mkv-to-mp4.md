## Why People Really Convert MKV to MP4

Most MKV conversions do not start with a format preference. They start with a blocked workflow: a file previews in VLC, then refuses to import into an editor, sync to a phone, cast to a TV, upload to a platform, or play for someone who just double-clicked it.

MKV is excellent as a storage container. It can hold multiple audio tracks, subtitles, chapters, metadata, high-bitrate video, and codec combinations that are common in archives and movie libraries. That same flexibility is why the file can become fragile outside power-user players.

MP4 is the safer handoff format. When the output uses H.264 video and AAC audio, it matches what most devices, browsers, editing apps, social platforms, and default media players expect. You are not making the file more technically impressive, you are making it easier for other software and people to accept.

## MKV vs MP4: Which One Should You Keep?

| Question | MKV | MP4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is it best at? | Keeping a rich original file with tracks, subtitles, chapters, and unusual codec combinations. | Being accepted by phones, browsers, upload forms, editors, TVs, and people who use default apps. |
| When does it feel great? | When you control the player, such as VLC, MPV, Plex, or a desktop archive workflow. | When the file has to leave your setup and work somewhere else without explanation. |
| What usually goes wrong? | The video may play in one app but fail in another because the app does not support the container, audio, subtitles, or codec mix. | Less flexible for complex track layouts, but much more predictable for playback and sharing. |
| Best codec pairing for compatibility | Can contain almost anything, including H.264, HEVC, VP9, AV1, MPEG-2, FLAC, DTS, AC-3, subtitles, and more. | H.264 video with AAC audio is the safest general-purpose target. |
| Quality impact | Great for preserving the source exactly as an archive. | Lossless if the streams are remuxed; slightly lossy if incompatible streams must be transcoded. |
| Best user moment | Keeping the master copy. | Sending, uploading, editing, previewing, syncing, casting, or handing off the video. |

A good workflow often keeps both: MKV as the archive, MP4 as the version that actually moves through the world.

## Remux First, Transcode Only When Needed

A lot of online converters treat every MKV like it needs a full conversion. That is wasteful. If the video is already H.264 and the audio is already AAC, the smart move is a remux: copy the existing streams into an MP4 container without decoding and compressing the video again.

A remux keeps the original visual quality because the frames are not re-encoded. It is also much faster, especially for long files, because the browser is mostly repackaging streams instead of rebuilding the video from scratch.

Transcoding is only for the cases where compatibility needs it. If the MKV contains streams that common MP4 workflows reject, GrepCut converts the main video and audio to a safer H.264/AAC MP4. That may take longer, but the result is the file most users came for: one that opens, imports, uploads, and plays.

## Common questions

### Is MKV to MP4 conversion lossless?

It is lossless when GrepCut can remux the file. That usually means the MKV already contains MP4-compatible streams such as H.264 video and AAC audio. In that case, GrepCut changes the container without re-encoding the frames. If the source uses incompatible codecs, it needs transcoding, which can be slightly lossy.

### Is my MKV uploaded to GrepCut?

No. The conversion runs locally in your browser with WebAssembly and WebCodecs. Your file stays on your device, which matters for private recordings, client videos, unreleased content, large files, and anything you do not want passing through a random upload server.

### Why does my MKV work in VLC but not on iPhone, Apple TV, or my smart TV?

VLC is unusually tolerant. Many default apps are not. Your device may support the video codec but reject the MKV container, or it may dislike the audio codec, subtitle type, metadata, or track layout. A simple MP4 with H.264/AAC is the safer compatibility target.

### Does GrepCut keep subtitles and extra audio tracks?

GrepCut focuses on creating one clean MP4 from the primary video and audio track. Embedded MKV subtitles, commentary tracks, alternate languages, chapters, and secondary audio tracks are not carried into the output MP4.

### Will converting MKV to MP4 make the file smaller?

Not always. A remux usually keeps the file size close to the original because the video and audio streams are copied. A transcode can make the file smaller or larger depending on the source codec, bitrate, resolution, duration, and browser encoder settings.

### Should I delete the original MKV after converting?

Keep the MKV if it is your master copy, especially if it contains subtitles, multiple audio tracks, chapters, or higher-quality source streams. Use the MP4 as the practical version for editing, uploading, sending, or playback on default devices.

### Can I edit the MP4 after converting?

Yes. After conversion, open the MP4 in the [GrepCut editor](/) to trim dead air, combine clips, add subtitles, remove backgrounds, resize for social, and export again in the same private browser workflow.
